Design a Board Game That Improves Second Language Learning

What if a game helped learners practise English and enjoy the challenge? Choose one language-learning difficulty, research existing games, and create your own physical or digital board game. Explain how the rules help players practise the language.

📌 What to Hand In

  • Report — 50%: Explain the learning difficulty, games you researched, your design choices, and what you changed after testing. Include rules and a source list.
  • Finished game — 25%: Make a playable physical or digital board game, with all materials, clear rules, and a way to check answers.
  • Presentation — 25%: Give a 5–10 minute talk. Explain the learning goal and demonstrate a short example turn.
  • Your own work: Create your own game content and design. Credit the games, ideas, facts, and images that inspired you.

🎲 Choose Your Game Format

  • Physical game: Use paper, cardboard, cards, dice, counters, or other simple materials. Make all the pieces players need.
  • Digital board game: Use a virtual board, digital cards, or another tool that clearly shows how to play. Test that players can use it.
  • Rules and answer support: Include a rule sheet and an answer key or guidance for checking open answers.
  • Simple tools are enough. The game should be clear, enjoyable, and connected to language practice.

🎯 Define Your Learning Goal

  • Choose one difficulty: For example, remembering words, making accurate sentences, or speaking with more confidence.
  • Name your players: Give their age group, English level, and the number of players.
  • State the aim: Explain what players will practise and how the game gives them repeated chances to use it.
  • Link other subjects if useful: You may include history, science, art, or another subject. Explain how it supports your learning goal.

🧩 Start with These Design Questions

1. What happens on a turn?

Does a player roll dice, move, draw a card, describe something, or make a sentence? Write the steps in order. Show when the player uses English.

2. Where does the learning happen?

What words or language structures will players practise? How will they check answers and learn from mistakes? Moving a counter alone is not language practice.

3. Why is it fun and fair?

Will players use choices, luck, teamwork, or strategy? How will everyone get a chance to practise? What happens if a player needs help?

4. How does it end?

Set a clear finishing rule, score system, and way to handle a tie. State an estimated playing time. Players should know what they are trying to achieve.

Research tip: Start by comparing 2–3 existing games, as a useful target. Explain how each works, what makes it enjoyable, and how it could support language learning. Use original rulebooks or official game information where possible. Save the creator or publisher, title, date, and link.

Show your original contribution: Explain the idea you borrowed, name its source, and describe what you changed. A new title or different colours alone do not show a new learning design.

📝 A Rule Sheet Players Can Follow

Include: Game name → Age and English level → Learning aim → Player numbers and estimated time → Materials → Set-up → Steps of a turn → Answer checking and help → Scoring → Ending and ties.

Example turn — a model to adapt: Draw a word card. Make a sentence using the word. Check the example answer or ask the checker to use the answer guide. If your sentence shows the meaning clearly, gain one point. If it needs a change, read the help card and try again.

Open answers: Speaking tasks may have several correct answers. Give examples and simple checking criteria instead of pretending there is only one answer. For example: “The meaning is clear, the target word is used correctly, and the answer is a complete sentence.”

Report plan: Title and your name → Introduction and learning difficulty → Research into existing games → Your players and learning goal → Game design and rules → Testing and improvements → Conclusion → Source list.

🔧 Test, Notice, Improve

A prototype is an early, simple version of your game. Test it before spending time on decoration. First play through the turns yourself. Then, if your teacher allows it, ask suitable players to try it. They can give feedback; the design and report remain your work.

Record: the test date, number of players, how long it took, a rule that caused confusion, examples of language practice, and the changes you made. Use anonymous labels such as Player A rather than personal details.

Simple testing note: “Players were unsure how to check a sentence. I added an example answer and a checking guide. In the next test, they could check answers with less help.”

Be honest about results: A short test can show that players understand the rules and practise English. It does not prove that their English level has improved. Explain what you observed and what would need more testing.

💡 Useful Language: Explaining Your Game and Choices

Use short commands for rules, clear linking words for the order of play, and reasons to explain your design decisions.

The aim of the game is to... This game is designed for... First,... Next,... Finally,... On your turn, draw / move / explain... If you answer correctly, you... This task helps players practise...

Rules: “Place the cards face down. Take turns drawing a card. If you are unsure, use the help card.” Use must for a requirement and can for an allowed choice.

Explain your choices: “I chose this task because learners need to use words in context.” “Unlike [game], my game asks players to...” “I was inspired by [game / creator], but I changed...”

Explain testing: “During the test, I noticed that...” “Players found ... difficult, so I...” “After feedback, I changed...” “This suggests that..., but more testing is needed to...”

📊 What Good Work Looks Like

Report — 50%

A clear learning difficulty and aim; useful research into existing games; reasons for your own choices; clear rules; honest testing notes; a logical structure; and accurate source credits.

Game — 25%

A playable, original game with accurate tasks, complete materials, clear rules, and suitable answer support. The game gives its target players meaningful chances to practise language.

Presentation — 25%

A clear 5–10 minute explanation and brief demonstration, useful visuals, short speaking notes, and answers showing that you understand the learning goal and design.

🛡️ Academic Honesty

Academic honesty means being clear about whose words, ideas, and work you use. Your project should show your understanding. Give credit for borrowed ideas, facts, exact words, pictures, charts, and videos. A source note tells the reader where information came from.

A key rule: Changing a few words, translating a text, or turning a quotation into reported speech does not make the idea yours. You still need to name the source. A source list at the end is not enough on its own: add a source note beside the borrowed information too.

📚 Keep useful source notes
  • When you research, save the author or organisation, title, date, link, and any useful page number or video timestamp.
  • Keep exact quotations in quotation marks in your notes. Put your own notes beside them so you can tell them apart later.
  • Use a short source note in the report, such as (Author, year). For an organisation's work, use its name. If no date is given, use n.d. (“no date”). Follow any format your teacher gives you.
  • At the end, list full source details. A simple web-source pattern is: Author or organisation. (Year or n.d.). Title. Website name. URL.
  • Match every source note to an entry in your source list. Use sources you have actually read or watched. Never invent an author, quotation, date, or link.
🖼️ Pictures, charts, videos, and help from others
  • Add credit beside a borrowed image, chart, or clip, and include the full source in your source list. For example: Image: [creator], [website], [year].
  • If you make a chart using someone else's data, write Chart by me; data from [source] ([year]).
  • Crediting an image does not automatically give you permission to reuse it. Use your own images or material that allows reuse, and follow its conditions.
  • For your own photos or drawings, you can write Photo/drawing by me. You do not need a research source for your own opinions or widely known facts.
  • Follow your teacher's rules about outside help and AI. If help is allowed, explain what help you used. Check facts against real sources and make sure you understand and can explain your final work.
